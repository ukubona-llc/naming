The Classical Counterpart

Bach, elevator music, and the MCRBO Pentad

The same vocabulary that decoded gospel turnarounds and Sondheim dissonances unlocked something much older. Suite No. 3 (in D, despite the dreaded "Air on the G String" moniker) is famous for being the ultimate sublime tension-and-release — essentially the world's most famous elevator music. But once the naming clicked, the veil lifted.

Bach — Orchestral Suite No. 3 in D Major
"Air" · Halidon Music[5]

Suddenly, I saw the song for what it mechanically is across its binary structure (A :|| B :||). The first half is a pristine, falling descent: I → I7 (3rd inversion) → vi → vi7 (3rd inversion).

But the second half (Part B) is where the real madness lies. Starting around 2:42, Bach unleashes a tremendous cluster of passing chords to execute a crazy, seamless chromatic bass ascent: G → G# → A → A# → B.

This chromaticism is achieved seamlessly through the use of first inversions of secondary dominants. The G# is the third of an Edom7/G# (resolving to A), and the A# is the third of an F#dom7/A# (resolving to B). It hit me like a lightning bolt: this is fucking black gospel. Bach would connect with a Hammond organ and play preacher chords behind a sermon any day without looking even slightly out of place.

Suddenly, I saw through Bach's counterpoint completely. Even the most subtle contrapuntal weaving wasn't a separate, inaccessible classical discipline—it all mapped perfectly into its assigned roles within the pentad: Bass → Chord → Melody (Piano can hold these three down on its own --and in that voicing!) → Rhythm → Other (These two are often handled by the rhythm section). The system held.

The Cost of Thinking

The Triage Layer: Eigengaps and the Price of Ambiguity

Ambiguity is not free. In computational systems, preserving it burns energy; in clinical systems, it burns time. If a system cannot distinguish between a problem that requires an immediate, low-entropy collapse and a problem that demands high-dimensional exploration, it will either prematurely collapse every complex problem or overthink every routine one.

This exact problem emerged when engineering the Game of Care (a clinical decision-support architecture). We realized that relying on a binary SlowAPI_Toggle = true/false was fundamentally flawed. Asking a Large Language Model (like Claude or GPT) to decide if a problem is complex requires the LLM to spend expensive tokens just to evaluate the complexity.

The architecture needed a cheap, upstream classifier—a Basal Ganglia layer—that could route the request *before* waking up the expensive Prefrontal Cortex (the LLM). It had to answer one question: Is the ambiguity epistemic or structural?

Epistemic Ambiguity

Missing Information

You don't know the answer because you lack data (e.g., guessing a stranger's gate at the airport). More signal fixes this. The loss landscape is steep. Action: Collapse fast. Use a strict, ATL-style o)-(o funnel.

Structural Ambiguity

Organized Multiplicity

Multiple equally coherent answers exist simultaneously because the system's symmetry preserves them (e.g., the rootless dom7♭9 chord, or a living-donor evaluation). The Eigengap Heuristic

How do you detect structural ambiguity cheaply? You use the eigengap heuristic. By sampling a few candidate completions and analyzing their clustering, you can estimate the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of the decision landscape without doing the expensive math.

If the system finds one massive cluster ($k=1$), the problem is a routine protocol (e.g., Sepsis). Route it low-effort. If the system finds a clean gap after four equally sized clusters ($k=4$), it has found a rootless dom7♭9. Route it high-effort. If there is no gap and just a diffuse smear of noise, the prompt is just underspecified.
